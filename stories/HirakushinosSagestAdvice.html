Hirakushino’s Sagest Advice

“And truly he did not have any words to say, for he was a fraud that never listened to his mentor, Master Hirakushino.”

“But Ephanes, what of Hariku? Was not he, too, a falsely talking ‘wise man’ who claimed to know?”

“Oh contrare, Mikonysus. In fact, he was a very wise man who knew many things, for he would speak and men would listen, and when they did, prosperity would drown them. For example, his sagest advice.”

“Now what is this I hear?”

“Oh? You have not heard it? Master Hirakushino’s sagest advice?”

“No, indeed I have not, so what is Master Hirakushino’s sagest advice?”

“Now it goes like this: but first you must know how it was that he told me this, where he brought me, and why he finally did it.

        I was a younger man, early in my years before thirty, but after twenty. His wooden temple in the forest looked out to a rainy day. I had woken up before him and was finishing my morning meditation. The green jungle receded from my sight as I stepped back into the toasty room. He had made me sleep there last night. He brought two cups of dazzling china, each with his good tea, though his with purple leaf soaked inside, set it down as he did a prayer, which I copied over in good observation. Then he took a sip, and so did I. For he had not spoken for some many days, he thusly cleared his throat.

        ‘Young Ephileithyio—and that was my name in those days—you have waited long and well before this story I shall tell, and within it a gem of knowledge, my sagest advice:

I was walking in the forest picking flowers

I was setting traps for rabbits in the woods

In the distance I saw a clearing, the warmness and the sun

In the trap laid it, unfurled, twisted on display

I walked out, to the golden grass

I walked on, a feast of plenty

In the center, a lying deer was shot and dying

In the mist I saw a doe,

It looked at me with solemn eyes

It learned time would seal Death’s present’s ties

I waited there for decades, though it all became a flash

The body decomposing, soon all the bones were showing

A flower sprout its bud, poking out of the eye, I picked it

Death done his good duty: destruction turned to beauty’

He clutched his heart then, as his eyes opened wide, I saw in them the whiteness, whiter than a beluga whale, as he fell over, dead.

I had his words and remembered it well, for he taught me how to commit to memory anything with precision, and because he had not spoken then for weeks, and because they were his last. I believe he wanted me to know it was something important, but to this very day I still cry at night in bed, and deny myself from worldly pleasures, such as sex and alcohol, for I have not understood what he meant to tell me. And now I give it to you, as my heavy heart grows tired at beating just to know.”

…

“And so now you want my days, too, to be spent in arduous pondering? Ha! But truly, it reminds me of this rhythm I once was given:

Left waiting on the track, a new toy for a little boy, the train turning ‘round the bend.

He saunters to it, awing death’s good gift. Soon, his watching sister thinks, he will meet his end.

Pulling hard, and crying harder, our boy catches Fate’s good deeds, his harmonious curls drawing near to perfect.

Sickly sister, and sprinting steel, running track, and spinning wheel, in between, his mushy mind, a sure fit.”
